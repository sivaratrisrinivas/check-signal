The goal isn't tokenmaxxing, or the practice of torching tokens for sport. Instead, he wants to give people room to experiment with radical new strategies and ways of working that, should they pan out, could vastly improve their productivity and more than justify the token costs.

Inside Every

Token budgets at the frontier

Dan’s encouragement to experiment comes with an expectation: When people spend company money, they should assess what that spending produced and whether they could be more efficient next time.

Turning that philosophy into spending decisions falls to head of operations Arielle Shipper. For now, she evaluates expensive runs case by case, using the results to decide when to keep funding the work and when to change course.

Her early-warning system is the company card. She keeps the ChatGPT automatic credit refill relatively low, so an unusually rapid succession of charges tells her that someone is building or testing something big.

When she’s alerted to changes over the set amount Arielle typically asks the team on Slack if anyone is in the middle of a particularly big run or check the usage leaderboard and message the people at the top. “Then Dan always responds, ‘It’s me running Ultra.’” she says, which explains why he’s always on the top of the leaderboard.

A common occurrence. (Screenshot courtesy of Laura Entis.)

She wants answers to three questions: What did the run cost? What did it buy us? And what did we learn?

The team has started offering these retrospectives unprompted. After a string of Astra experiments racked up billions of tokens, head of video Randy Counsman messaged her detailing what had worked, what hadn’t, and what he’d learned—which, it turns out, was a lot.

More on that below.

Data point

4.5 billion

That’s how many OpenAI tokens Randy burned through in an attempt to make a 3D model of his face with Astra.

The project started innocently enough: Inspired by social media posts of impressive-looking AI-generated demos made with Blender, a free 3D tool, he wanted to try it himself. Experimenting with AI, after all, is an important component of his job.

Using strategies shared on X, he set up an orchestrator agent to maintain the plan, an implementer to assign tasks, and subagents to execute them, and instructed Codex to keep looping through improvements until the model was “implemented very well,” Randy says. In retrospect, “It was an ambiguous goal.”

Then he let the model run. And run. And run.

By the time he pressed pause on the project, the token spend was in the billions and the result was, as he puts it, “very janky.”

The output, rendered in four different shaders. (Image courtesy of Randy Counsman.)

An AI audit of his Codex sessions revealed some glaring inefficiencies: Layers of agents passed growing amounts of context back and forth, including messages checking whether a subagent had completed a task. He’d unwittingly created a compute-hungry “unruly swarm” of agents.

Randy has since rebuilt his project setup. He dropped the implementer, caped the orchestrator at five Sol subagents to control costs, and built in explicit feedback checkpoints. For a recent project, he also generated an image of the design he wanted, so a judge agent could check the model's work against a concrete target instead of an open-ended instruction to keep improving.

A failed experiment is still a good investment if it shows where an AI system falls short. Randy now has a benchmark to run new models against—how well they turn an organic 2D image into an organic 3D model—and a leaner setup to get a better result with fewer tokens.

Token Math: feedback on the hed, dek, and opening

Bottom line

The piece has a real hook, and the current package buries it. The hook is that the person at the top of the usage leaderboard, by more than 3x, is the one telling everyone else to spend more. Neither hed says so. The dek doesn't either. The opening paragraph gets there only in sentence 2, after a slack first line. I'd move the paradox into the hed, use the dek for the guardrail ("worth it" vs. "just tokenmaxxing"), and open on the stat.

The strongest idea

The top spender is the one arguing for more spending, and the reason is ambition, not tokens. The draft's own line, "the size of the gap has him wondering whether the rest of us are being ambitious enough with AI," is the sharpest sentence in the opening. It reframes a cost story as an ambition story, and it sets up everything after it: the "not tokenmaxxing" caveat, Arielle's three questions, and Randy's 4.5-billion-token flop that still paid off.

A close second is the sequence in paragraph 4: experiment first, "only then do the token math." That's the piece's actual thesis, and the current hed only gestures at it.

Headline

Original: "Token Math"

  • It's a label, not a claim. It reads like a section slug and would be a weak email subject line.
  • Its payoff ("only then do the token math") doesn't arrive until paragraph 4.
  • Keep the phrase, but use it in the body or as a section tag, not as the hed.

Alt: "How Many Tokens are Too Many?"

  • It's a better subject line than the original, but it has two problems.
  • It leans toward restraint, while the piece's surprise runs the other way (the top user wants more). It undersells the story.
  • It promises a threshold the piece never gives. The body offers a method (cost, what it bought, what you learned), not a number.
  • Style nit: "Are" is a verb and should be capitalized in title case.

Recommended hed: "Our CEO Wants You to Burn More Tokens" (37 characters)

  • It states the surprising claim and fits an email subject line.
  • It's bold on purpose. The qualifications ("should consider," "not tokenmaxxing," "case by case") belong in the dek and body, and they're already there.
  • The risk is that it reads as an endorsement of tokenmaxxing. The dek below handles that.

Other options

  • "How Many Tokens Are Too Few?" flips the alt hed to match the piece's real direction. It's a question, but a more surprising one.
  • "Spend First, Do the Token Math Later" keeps the original phrase and states the thesis. It's slightly riskier, since it can sound like permission to be reckless. The body's guardrails (retrospectives, card-refill alerts, Marcus's usage cap) do temper it.

Dek

Original: "What's the ROI on experimentation?"

  • It's a question that repeats the hed's question form and adds no detail or implication.
  • "ROI on experimentation" is abstract. It doesn't say what's being experimented with, what's at stake, or who's spending.

Alt: "When expensive experimentation is worth it and when to rein your tokenmaxxing in."

  • This is the better of the two. It has a clear implication and both sides of the tradeoff, and the "rein in" echo is nice.
  • It pairs well with the recommended hed above.
  • It's a little stiff ("expensive experimentation"). "Your tokenmaxxing" also accuses the reader.
  • "Tokenmaxxing" isn't defined until paragraph 2. That's probably fine for Every's audience, but the dek shouldn't be the reader's first encounter with a term the piece has to explain.
  • If the opening keeps "reining yourself in," the dek repeats it. My rewrite below drops that phrase from the opening.

Recommended dek: "When an expensive AI experiment is worth it—and when you're just tokenmaxxing."

If the hed stays a question or a label, use this stat-forward dek instead:
"Dan Shipper uses more than three times as many OpenAI tokens as anyone else at Every. The real test isn't the bill—it's what the spend produced."

Opening paragraph

What's working

  • The inversion is right: the top spender says spend more.
  • "Should consider ramping up" is appropriately hedged.

What's not

  1. Sentence 1 is the weakest in the draft. "Often if you are the highest spender at your company you might think about reining yourself in" has three hedges in a row ("often," "if," "might"). It's missing a comma after "company" and it's generic. It spends the most valuable line on a premise the reader already assumes.
  2. The stat is buried in sentence 3. "More than three times as many tokens as the next-highest user" is the concrete, surprising fact, and it should land in the first sentence.
  3. "Spender" and "consumes tokens" mix units. "Spender" implies dollars, but the leaderboard measures tokens. Pick one: "user" or "consumer."

  6. The end of the paragraph is good, but the draft leans on it too little. "Whether the rest of us are being ambitious enough" is the payoff and should stay last.
